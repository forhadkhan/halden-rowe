Closing costs are the fees and prepaid items you pay when a home purchase closes, on top of your down payment. Buyers are often surprised by them. A common rule of thumb puts a buyer's closing costs at 2 to 5 percent of the purchase price, but the real figure depends on your state, your lender, your loan and the home itself.

You see every one of them in writing well before closing day, which leaves time to question them.

## Where you will see them

If you are buying with a mortgage, two federal forms do most of the work.

The **Loan Estimate** comes from your lender within three business days of your loan application. It lists the expected interest rate, monthly payment and closing costs.

The **Closing Disclosure** arrives at least three business days before closing. It shows the final figures.

The two forms use the same layout. Put them side by side and compare each line. If a number has moved, ask your lender why before closing day, not at the table.

## The four groups of charges

### Lender charges

These are what the lender charges to make the loan. They can include an origination or underwriting fee and a credit report fee. They may also include **discount points**: an optional upfront payment that lowers your interest rate. One point is 1 percent of the loan amount. Points make sense if you expect to keep the loan long enough for the lower payment to repay the upfront cost, and your lender can show you where that break-even falls.

### Third-party services

These are paid to the companies that check the property and the title:

- **Appraisal**, which the lender orders to confirm the home's value.
- **Title search and title insurance.** The lender will usually require a policy that protects the lender. A separate owner's policy protects you, and in many places it is optional. Ask what it covers before you decline it.
- **Settlement or escrow fee**, paid to the title or escrow company, or the closing attorney, that handles the money and the paperwork.
- **Survey or pest inspection**, required in some areas and for some loans.

### Government fees

Your county charges a fee to record the deed and the mortgage. Many states and some cities also charge a **transfer tax** when a property changes hands. Who pays it depends on local custom and on what you negotiate. Some states, Texas among them, have no transfer tax at all.

### Prepaid items and escrow

These are not fees. They are costs of owning the home that you pay in advance:

- the first year of **homeowners insurance**
- a few months of **property tax** and insurance, placed in an escrow account the lender uses to pay those bills
- **prepaid interest** from the closing date to the end of that month
- **homeowners association** dues or transfer fees, if the home is in an association

Because prepaid interest runs to the end of the month, closing late in a month lowers the cash you need at closing. It doesn't change the total interest you pay over the life of the loan.

## What is not a closing cost

Your **down payment** is separate. So is your **earnest money deposit**, the sum you put down when your offer is accepted. The deposit is credited toward what you owe at closing.

The figure that matters on the day is **cash to close**, shown on the Closing Disclosure. It is your down payment plus closing costs, minus your deposit and any credits from the seller or lender.

## Where you can save

**Shop the services the lender lets you choose.** The Loan Estimate lists services you can shop for, which often include title and settlement services. Get a second quote.

**Compare lenders on the same day.** Ask two or three lenders for a Loan Estimate for the same loan amount and type on the same day, so the rates are comparable.

**Ask about seller credits.** In some markets, buyers negotiate a credit from the seller toward closing costs, usually in exchange for a higher price or fewer repair requests. Your loan type may cap how much credit you can receive, so check with your lender first.

**Question anything you don't recognize.** Every line on the Closing Disclosure should have an explanation. Your lender and your agent can tell you what it is and whether it is negotiable.

## A warning about wiring money

Criminals target home buyers with fake emails that look like they come from a title company or agent, asking you to wire your closing funds to a new account. Before you send any money, call your title or escrow company on a phone number you have checked independently, not one from the email, and confirm the wiring instructions. Your agent will never ask you to change where you send closing funds by email.

## Before your closing

Read your Closing Disclosure the day it arrives. Compare it with your Loan Estimate, list your questions and send them to your lender and your agent together. Three business days is enough time to resolve most questions, but only if you start on the first day.

This article is general information, not legal, tax or lending advice. Rules and customs vary by state, so ask your lender, closing attorney or title company about your own purchase.
