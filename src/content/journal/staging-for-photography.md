For most buyers, the listing photos are the first viewing. They decide from a phone screen whether your home is worth an afternoon, so the hour the photographer spends in each room matters. You don't need new furniture to get good photos. You need clear surfaces, good light and a week of small jobs.

This is the plan I give sellers, in the order I'd do it.

## A week before: clear and repair

Start by taking things away. A camera flattens a room and makes every object compete for attention, so a room that feels lived-in to you can read as cluttered in a photograph.

- **Clear surfaces.** Kitchen counters, bathroom shelves, dressers and side tables should hold one or two things each, or nothing.
- **Thin the shelves.** Take out about half of what's on open shelving, then arrange what's left with space around each piece.
- **Put away personal items.** Photographs, letters, children's drawings, certificates, anything with your name on it. Buyers should be able to picture their own things in the room.
- **Remove spare furniture.** If you walk around a chair to cross a room, take the chair out. A friend's garage or a storage unit for a month is often worth it.
- **Fix small things.** Replace dead bulbs, tighten loose handles, touch up scuffed paint and re-caulk a stained tub edge. The camera finds them all.

When you replace bulbs, use the same color in every fixture in a room. A mix of warm and cool white looks patchy in photos, and it is hard to correct afterwards.

## The day before: room by room

**Kitchen.** Clear the counters completely, then put back one thing, such as a bowl of lemons or a cutting board. Take the magnets and papers off the fridge. Hide the dish soap, sponges and dish towels.

**Living room.** Plump and straighten the cushions, fold any throws, and square the rug to the sofa. Remove remote controls, chargers and cables. If the fireplace works, clean the hearth.

**Bedrooms.** Make the beds with plain, pressed bedding and two pillows each. Clear the bedside tables down to a lamp. Close closet doors.

**Bathrooms.** Remove everything from the counter and the shower. Hang fresh, plain towels, close the toilet lid and take away the bath mat.

**Outside.** Mow the lawn, sweep the paths and porch, and put the trash cans out of sight. Move cars off the driveway and away from the front of the house.

**Pets.** Put away bowls, beds, toys and litter boxes, and plan for the animals to be out of the house during the shoot.

## On the day: light

Open every blind and curtain fully, and turn on every light, including lamps and the lights under kitchen cabinets. Photographers usually shoot with both daylight and interior lights on, because it evens out dark corners.

Ask your photographer when the light suits the front of your house. A house that faces west may photograph best in the late afternoon. Many photographers also offer a dusk exterior, taken just after sunset with every interior light on, which works well for homes with large windows.

## Keep your home secure

Listing photos are public, and they stay online after the sale. Before the shoot, put away:

- jewelry, cash and small valuables
- mail, bills and anything showing your name or account numbers
- prescription medicines
- spare keys, and any note of an alarm code or Wi-Fi password

Take down or cover anything that shows your daily routine, such as a calendar or a school schedule.

## What not to do

Don't use photos to hide a problem. A buyer who arrives to find the stained ceiling you kept out of frame trusts the rest of your listing less, and that costs more at the negotiation than the stain would have.

Don't let the photos promise more than the house. Mild color correction is normal. Replacing a gray sky with a blue one, removing a power line or widening a room is not. If you use virtual staging, with furniture added digitally to an empty room, label those photos clearly in the listing so nobody arrives expecting a sofa that isn't there.

## A short checklist

On the morning of the shoot, walk through each room once more and check:

- counters and tables nearly bare
- beds made, towels hung, toilet lids closed
- every light on and every blind open
- no cars, bins, hoses or pet things in sight
- nothing personal or valuable on show

Then leave the photographer to work. Most shoots go faster, and look better, without the owner moving things back into the frame. If you'd like a second opinion first, your agent can walk the rooms with you a few days before the shoot.
