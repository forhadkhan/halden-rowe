A listing is a sales document. Most listings are accurate, but every one is written to make you want to visit, and the useful information sits in the details rather than the headline. This is how I read a listing before a viewing, and what I ask once I'm inside.

## Start with the facts row

Price, bedrooms, bathrooms and floor area are the numbers everyone reads first. Each one deserves a second look.

**Floor area.** Ask where the figure came from. It might be the agent's own measurement, the owner's estimate, an appraisal or public tax records, and these can disagree for the same house. Some figures include a finished basement or an enclosed porch; others leave them out. If you are comparing two homes by price per square foot, check that both figures were measured the same way.

**Bedrooms.** Local building codes usually require a bedroom to meet a minimum size and to have a window large enough to climb out of in an emergency. A room that falls short may be listed as a den, an office or a bonus room. It can still be a perfectly good room, but it may count differently when the home is appraised.

**Bathrooms.** A half bath has a toilet and a sink but no shower or tub. So "3.5 baths" means three full bathrooms and one half bath.

**Year built.** Age tells you what to look for. A house from the 1920s may still have original wiring, pipes or windows; a house from the 2010s is more likely to need a new water heater than a new roof. Ask what has been replaced and when, and ask whether the work had permits.

## Read the photos as evidence

Listing photos are chosen to flatter. A wide-angle lens makes rooms look larger than they feel, and bright daytime shots say little about how a north-facing room feels on a winter afternoon.

The most useful thing to notice is what's missing. If there is no photo of the second bathroom, the basement or the back of the house, ask why. Sometimes the answer is dull, such as a tenant's belongings or a room mid-repair. Sometimes it changes your mind about visiting.

## Notice the words

Listing language aims to be both true and appealing. "Cozy" often means small. "Original details" can mean an original kitchen. "Close to transit" can mean you will hear it. Treat these as prompts for questions, not as warnings.

Pay attention to what the description doesn't mention. If parking, laundry or outdoor space isn't listed, assume it isn't there until you've asked.

## Questions to ask at the viewing

Bring a list and write the answers down. A good agent will tell you what they know and say plainly when they don't.

- How long has the home been on the market, and has the price changed?
- What are the annual property taxes? Is there a homeowners association or building fee, and what does it cover?
- How old are the roof, the heating and cooling system and the water heater?
- Have there been leaks, flooding or insurance claims?
- Which work was done with permits?
- Which items stay with the house: appliances, light fixtures, window coverings?
- What do the utility bills come to in summer and in winter?
- Has the seller prepared disclosures, and can I read them before I make an offer?

Disclosure rules differ from state to state, so ask your own agent what the seller is required to tell you where you are buying.

## Use the senses a photo can't

Some things only show up in person. Smell each room, especially basements and closets, for damp. Look at the ceilings under bathrooms for water stains, and open the cupboard under the kitchen sink. Run a tap and flush a toilet to get a feel for the water pressure. Open and close a few windows and doors. Check your phone signal in the rooms where you would work.

Then stand still for a minute in the main bedroom and listen. Traffic, neighbors and the building's own systems are easier to hear when nobody is talking.

## Go back at a different time

If you are serious about a home, visit a second time at a different hour. A street that is quiet at eleven in the morning can be busy at half past five, and a sunny living room at noon may be dim by four. Walk to the nearest grocery store or transit stop yourself rather than relying on the listing's description.

## After the viewing

Write your notes the same day, before the homes you have seen start to blur together. Note what you liked, what worried you and what you still need to find out.

A viewing does not replace a professional inspection. It helps you decide whether a home is worth an offer and an inspector's fee. When you're ready, your agent can walk you through how an offer works where you are buying, including the deposit, the inspection period and the timeline to closing.
