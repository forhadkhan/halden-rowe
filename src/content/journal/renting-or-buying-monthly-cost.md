People often ask whether renting is "throwing money away" or buying is the bigger risk. Neither slogan helps. The better question is what each option costs you every month once you count everything, and what you get back. This guide shows how to line the two up.

## Start with the full cost of owning

A mortgage payment is only part of what a homeowner pays each month. Count these:

- **Principal and interest**, the payment to your lender.
- **Property tax.** Rates vary a lot by state and county, so look up the tax on the specific home.
- **Homeowners insurance**, which your lender will require.
- **Mortgage insurance**, if your down payment is small. Your lender can tell you whether it applies.
- **Association dues**, for a condo or a home in an association.
- **Maintenance and repairs.** A common rule of thumb sets aside about 1 percent of the home's price each year. Older homes may need more, and a new condo may need less.

Property tax and insurance are often collected with your mortgage payment. Maintenance is not, so it is the line people forget.

## Count the full cost of renting too

Rent is simpler, but the lease number is not the whole cost:

- **Monthly rent**, and how much it may rise at renewal. Ask about the building's history and read the renewal terms.
- **Renters insurance**, which is usually inexpensive and often required by the landlord.
- **Utilities and fees** that the lease passes on to you, such as parking, pets or amenities.
- **The deposit.** You get it back if you leave the home in good condition, but it ties up cash meanwhile. Our rental listings state the deposit in their rental terms, so you can see it before you inquire.

## Account for your cash and your equity

Two items sit outside the monthly bill but belong in the comparison.

**Cash up front.** Buying takes a down payment and closing costs. Renting takes a deposit and the first month's rent. That cash is yours either way, and what you would earn on it elsewhere is a real cost of buying. If it would otherwise sit in a savings account, the cost is the interest it would have earned.

**Equity.** Part of each mortgage payment pays down your loan, and that part builds your ownership stake. It is not a cost in the way rent is. Interest, tax, insurance and repairs are costs. Only the principal comes back to you, and only if you sell for enough to cover your loan and the costs of selling.

## A worked example

These numbers are illustrative round figures, chosen to show the method. They are not quotes, and they do not describe any home we list. Replace each one with your own.

Say a home costs $500,000 and a comparable rental costs $2,500 a month. You put 20 percent down ($100,000) and borrow $400,000 over 30 years at 6 percent.

| Monthly item | Own | Rent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rent | | $2,500 |
| Principal and interest | $2,400 | |
| Property tax (1% a year) | $417 | |
| Homeowners or renters insurance | $150 | $20 |
| Maintenance (1% a year) | $417 | |
| Interest on the $100,000 you could have kept (4% a year) | $333 | |
| **Total** | **$3,717** | **$2,520** |

On those figures, owning costs about $1,200 more each month. Now take back the equity. In the first month, about $2,000 of the $2,400 payment is interest and about $400 reduces your loan. That principal is money you still own. Subtract it and owning comes to roughly $3,300 a month, still above rent.

Change one input and the answer can move. If you stay a long time, the principal you pay down adds up, and rent may rise. If rents in your area are far higher than $2,500 for a similar home, the gap closes or flips. Run the table again with your local rent, tax and rate.

## The question the table cannot answer

How long will you stay? Buying has costs at both ends: closing costs when you buy, and agent and closing costs when you sell. Spread over one or two years, those costs are heavy. Spread over many years, they matter less. Our article on [closing costs](../understanding-closing-costs/) covers the buying side line by line.

Also ask how much flexibility you need. A lease ends on a known date. A home takes time to sell. Neither is better in general, and it depends on whether your job, family or plans are likely to change soon.

## A short checklist

1. Write down the full monthly cost of owning, including maintenance.
2. Write down the full monthly cost of renting, including renewal increases.
3. Add the interest your down payment would earn if you kept it.
4. Subtract the principal you would pay down, and label it equity, not savings.
5. Decide how many years you expect to stay.
6. Check that you would still have a cash cushion after buying.
